Anatomy of a Generation

From the terrain to the last roof: understand what each stage builds, why shapes vary, and where to look when a map needs diagnosis.

Overview

A map is a set of dependencies

Water constrains sites, sites call for roads, and roads organize quarters. Buildings arrive once available land and access have been defined.

The seed initializes deterministic random streams: each family of elements has its own substreams. Identical settings reconstruct the same geometric data. The palette, zoom and pins belong to the display and do not reshape the town.

Three composition workflows

Environment: terrain and natural cover, without settlements or human cultivation. Automatic: a regional hierarchy calculated from the terrain. List: the first place is the main settlement; subsequent places inherit the general theme and can have their own settings.

Geometry and presentation

The World model contains terrain, sites, roads, water, urban layers, land use and names. SVG and Canvas share the same data. A painted forest is a visual motif; a river or physical wall is a generation constraint.

01 · The physical foundation

Terrain, shoreline, drainage and rivers

  1. Build elevations. Noise at multiple scales produces an elevation grid. The chosen relief shapes a plain, hills, a valley or mountains. An imported heightmap replaces the procedural base.
  2. Add the sea. The coastal side and sea level determine submerged cells; the landform is oriented toward the coast. Contours become polygons for later stages.
  3. Resolve drainage. Depression filling and flow directions allow the water received by each cell to accumulate. Slopes and distance to water are reused downstream.
  4. Trace watercourses. Inflow from the map edge guarantees the requested main course. The route favors low ground; its bends are smoothed at a physical scale. Local sources remain small brooks; wider courses receive an external catchment.
  5. Form water surfaces. Widths, confluences, banks, lakes and estuaries are converted into geometry, then a water mask. Width increases with contributing area, particularly after a confluence.

Diagnosis: isolate terrain and water first. An inconsistent road or site may result from an unexpected water mask or slope.

02 · Where to settle?

Plausible centers, then a regional hierarchy

A settlement center is a site point, not necessarily the geometric center of its future outline. Selection combines dry, reasonably flat land, centrality, water access, crossings, harbor shelter and defensibility. Culture weights these preferences.

Eight site archetypes

Bridge, confluence, meander, harbor, estuary, valley, hilltop, plain. Auto chooses among available opportunities. An explicit choice attempts the requested type; an unsuitable manual position moves toward usable land.

Keep settlements apart

The planner reserves footprints and a margin around them, spaces centers by population, and ranks the remaining sites. Larger settlements precede smaller ones in count-based composition.

In automatic mode, counts depend on usable land area and relief; they produce a readable hierarchy without representing every historical farm. List mode preserves population, culture, site, position and individual properties. Converting an automatic region into a list preserves settlement generation keys and their random streams.

03 · Connect settlements

Roads, junctions and crossings

Regional roads use pathfinding on a cost grid. Slopes increase effort; water is an obstacle except at allowed crossings. Joining an existing road is favored, allowing routes to merge instead of drawing an independent path for every destination.

Approaches from map edges join the main center. The regional network then connects other settlements; major roads, minor roads and tracks differ in rank and width. Junctions are processed so the lines form a readable network.

Bridges and fords

A crossing has a deck aligned with the passage and a physical width. Urban bridges are reserved in relation to streets; small watercourses can receive footbridges and fords. Culture changes their architecture: timber, arches, Japanese bridges or canal bridges.

What to inspect

Follow a road from one center to the next. Look for an abrupt ending, a poorly connected junction or a crossing offset from the river. The shared network does not guarantee an exact number of connections for each place.

roads controls the number of regional exits; zero leaves it automatic. Internal streets belong to the urban partition in the next stage, rather than the regional road tracing alone.

04 · From land to roof

Divide the ground, preserve access

The urban engine works by “partitioning.” Streets and landmarks remove pieces of land; plots divide what remains.

  1. Resolve the cultural plan. The nucleus, growth phases, morphologies, enclosures and density parameters determine the construction scenario.
  2. Define the serviced footprint. Population, sprawl, access costs, terrain and water limit its area. Extensions follow available approaches.
  3. Create quarters. Primary roads, radials, rings, axes or grids divide the footprint. Older boundaries may survive as streets or walls.
  4. Reserve major elements. Castles, religious landmarks, palaces, ports, public spaces and activities claim lots before housing. Their access can reshape the cuts.
  5. Divide blocks. Secondary streets follow a direction field, cultural orientations and slopes. Alleys or passages make deeper pieces accessible.
  6. Divide plots and courtyards. Street frontage, depth, cultural type and density guide the divisions. Courtyards and gardens remain explicit parts of the ground.
  7. Build. Housing occupies its plot according to its typology: street frontage, courtyard house, pavilions, dispersed housing or a specialized form. Final corrections check access, density and edge roofs.

Street forms

Organic: curves, forks and small squares. Grid: regular axes and blocks. Rings: growth around a nucleus. Cul-de-sacs: branching access to deep plots. Terraces: contour streets and sloping approaches. These operators combine according to the culture.

Housing forms

Narrow, deep plots, built rows, enclosed courtyard houses, pavilions around open space, longhouses, roundhouses, mounds and tents. Phase age, density and sprawl settings vary coverage and frontage continuity.

Small towns may take the form of a linear village or clustered settlement rather than a miniature city. Camp cultures use a specialized engine of yards, rings or housing groups.

Enclosures, ditches and fringes

Physical walls remain visible: single or double enclosures, palisades, ramparts or cultural variants. Gates connect radials; towers, thickness and exterior reserves are part of their geometry. A wet ditch is a separate reserve, allowed only when water and terrain are suitable; gates and roads retain dry passages.

Suburbs grow along roads and can thicken into quarters; informal settlements occupy less valued land with their own grain. An open town uses a gradual fringe: its administrative boundary is not a wall drawn around the buildings.

The inhabited landscape

Fields, gardens, woodland and natural environments

After regional composition, land use combines distance to settlements, access, slope, height above water and soil variation. Gardens and orchards appear near inhabited places; fields, meadows, pastures, commons, woodland and marsh occupy compatible terrain. Fields can subdivide into groups of strips aligned with paths or terrain; farms and access are reserved in the countryside.

Urban ground is not always a green garden: natural ground, bare courtyards, paved spaces or gardens depend on biome and occupation type. Irrigated gardens remain possible. Painted brushes change visible motifs, not land classes or generated shapes.

Mixes and phases

cultureMix allows a second culture: growth by phases, sectors or continuous interpolation. Numeric parameters can interpolate; discrete choices retain their operators' logic. An explicit plan can replace the nucleus and phase recipe. Settlement-specific settings override inherited properties for that place.

These combinations are not a historical simulation. Some interactions remain reported in BUGS.md, including elven mixes and buildings at quarter boundaries.

The classes in this table are those of the current planner. They differ from the older size buttons hamlet / village / town / city / capital, which also control legacy parameters.

By default, a main settlement with more than 40,000 inhabitants uses the macro engine: major roads, quarters, enclosures and landmarks appear first. Quarter detail then arrives according to the view. Specialized camps retain their own path. Above 50,000 inhabitants in total, secondary settlements are also detailed on demand unless an internal setting overrides this.

Stable quarter keys make detail reproducible regardless of calculation order. An explicit extent and an imported image retain their scale; a large procedural population can enlarge the map automatically. Option limits are 600–40,000 m per side and 10–5,000,000 inhabitants.

Requested population is a form and density parameter, not an individual census. A distant view can show provisional fabric or fewer details without implying generation failure.
Presenting the model

Names, palettes, motifs and exports

Names are generated after geometry according to a language family linked to the culture or explicitly selected. A typed name replaces the displayed place name and some derived names without moving the settlement.

Available styles are parchment, atlas, watabou, engraving, cadastre, blueprint, illuminated, topographic and night. They change paper, ink, colors, shadows, water, roofs, typography and ornaments. Contours, labels, legend and the land-use layer are presentation controls. Classic textures are vector-based; painted motifs use embedded images, with vector fallback when needed.

Rendering layers terrain, cover and water, roads, urban ground, buildings, landmarks, defenses, crossings and annotations according to the backend. SVG retains vector output; Canvas supports interaction and large scenes. PNG captures an image; JSON exposes geometry layers. Exports retain decorative panels, while compact interactive views can move the title and legend into Map info.

The Map ID contains the seed and effective settings. The link adds view state, pins and brushes. The imported heightmap remains outside the link. Zoom, rotation and pins do not affect the generated model.

Diagnostic workshop

Break generation into four stages

Developer mode in Customize exposes a discreet panel for following construction after an empty map: 1 · rivers → 2 · settlement centers → 3 · roads → 4 · quarters and buildings.

Open the small Developer disclosure at the bottom of Customize, then choose Create empty map to apply the draft and start again from terrain. The four buttons become available in sequence. A new generation or settings change invalidates the session; previously calculated stages are retained while the session remains valid. Points at stages 2 and 3 are read-only overlays. Large maps start deferred detail only after stage 4.

Keeping the seed and settings fixed helps distinguish the appearance of a physical, siting, network or urban subdivision problem. This sequence is a diagnostic tool; the final stage's internal details remain the full partition described above.

The diagnostic empty map contains terrain and coast; stage 1 adds hydrology, rivers and lakes. Stage 4 also builds the countryside and names. Secondary positions may differ from automatic mode: diagnosis reserves a projected footprint before the main town, while the normal pipeline uses its already built footprint. The Map ID encodes settings without the intermediate diagnostic stage; it opens the normal map. A diagnostic JSON export retains developer.stage and the composition warning.

Create a reproducible case in a few steps

  1. Keep the Map ID or link of the displayed map and note the diagnostic stage.
  2. Locate the area: coordinates in meters, origin at the top left, x rightward and y downward.
  3. Add a pin with a specific note: clipped building, isolated road, discontinuous river, etc.
  4. Copy the bug report to include options, view and pins; a screenshot alone does not contain all generation inputs.
  5. Compare the same place after the next stage, then at the same zoom with another style if the issue seems visual.

The statistics ms.height, ms.hydrology, ms.rivers, ms.site, ms.roads, ms.urbanTotal, ms.planner, ms.network, ms.landuse and ms.names locate the automatic pipeline's cost. A cache can reuse terrain or the main town; comparing durations therefore requires distinguishing fresh calculation, cache reuse and deferred detail.
